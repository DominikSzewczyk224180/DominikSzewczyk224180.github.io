Intelligent Pickup Prediction System

A machine learning solution for logistics optimisation

De Rooy plans its deliveries in advance, but over 82% of its pickups arrive unexpected: same-day requests that land after the morning route plan is already set. I built a multi-model pipeline that forecasts where, when, and how large those pickups will be, so trucks can plan for them in advance. It is now live at De Rooy, with the predictions feeding straight into their route planning.

01 / the problem

De Rooy Transport plans its deliveries in advance with PTV route-planning software, but pickups are a different story. From January 2024 to June 2025, over 82% of pickups were unexpected: same-day requests that land after the morning plan is already set. Because they cannot be planned for, trucks handle them on the way back, doubling back over areas they already served and adding an estimated 15 to 25% extra kilometres, with the fuel, cost, and emissions that come with it. Digging into De Rooy's operational data, I found that these pickups actually cluster in predictable patterns despite arriving late, which opened the door to forecasting them before the day begins.

02 / the models

The system works in two stages across a 5 km grid over the Netherlands. First it predicts which cells will get a pickup, then four specialised models fill in the details for each of those cells:

  • Pickup occurrence: a Keras neural network that classifies each 5 km grid cell, reaching 90.2% accuracy, 88.9% precision, 84.2% recall, and an 86.5% F1-score, beating LSTM and other networks that stalled near or below 70 to 80%.
  • Exact location: a random forest that picks the most likely spot from De Rooy's history of past pickups, with a mean error of just 18 metres and 100% of predictions within 1 km.
  • Size and quantity: random forest regressors estimating loading metres and the number of shipping units, so planners can tell a small job from a large one.
  • Insertion time: an XGBoost regressor predicting when each pickup should slot into the day, with a mean error of about one hour.
  • Feature engineering: spatial, calendar, holiday, and lagged historical features (1, 2, 7, and 30 days back) that keep the models robust, including on holidays and peak days.

03 / results and impact

To measure the payoff, I compared optimised routes, with predicted pickups planned in from the start, against the original routes for real days. Two sample days saved 727 km and 485 km, with matching cuts in fuel, CO2, and driver time; the busier of the two came to about 946 kg of CO2 and over 7 hours saved. Across a full day, the simulation showed the tool could save up to around 1,500 km and 900 kg of CO2, depending on pickup volume.

De Rooy has since put the system into production. Rather than the dashboard, the pickup predictions now flow directly into their transport management system, so planners benefit from them without changing their routine. It is still early for confirmed real-world figures, but the direction is clear. The project was also featured in AI in de logistieke sector, a logistics-industry whitepaper, with a two-page case study on the system I delivered for De Rooy.

04 / what I did

I owned the project end to end: analysing the raw operational data, engineering the features, training and evaluating every model, and building the tool the results live in. I also translated the technical findings into plain language for planners, the IT team, and management, so the work could actually be adopted.
